Tiered greens turn a normal lag putt into a small geometry exam with emotional consequences.

You are not just reading left versus right anymore.
You are reading:

- what happens before the ridge
- what happens at the ridge
- and what happens after the ball changes levels and starts behaving like it found a second opinion

That is why golfers butcher these putts.

They stand behind the ball, picture one giant curve, hit one generic speed, and then act surprised when the ball either dies on the upslope or runs six feet by after it catches the lower tier and starts freelancing.

Tiered greens are not impossible.
They just punish lazy pace planning harder than flat greens do.

## The Rule First: Play the First Landing Zone, Not the Whole Putt

On a tiered green, I do **not** want your brain trying to solve the whole putt at once.

I want you to solve:

1. the **first landing zone**
2. the **speed at the crest or drop**
3. the **release on the new level**

That is the whole system.

Most tiered-green disasters happen because golfers skip step one and aim like the hole is the first important destination.

It is not.

On these greens, the first important destination is usually:

- the top of the ridge
- one to three feet over the ridge
- or the exact entry lane onto the next tier

If you pick that zone first, the read gets quieter fast.

## Use Three Tiered-Green Speed Windows

These are the speed windows I actually trust.

### 1. Putting up a tier

If the ball has to climb a ridge or shelf, I want the ball to **finish 18 to 30 inches past** if it reaches the hole.

Why that much?

Because dying it exactly at the crest is coward putting with architecture vocabulary.

On a true uphill tier change, a lot of misses never really had enough gas. They looked careful. They were just under-hit.

### 2. Putting down a tier

If the ball has to trickle over a ridge and release onto a lower section, I want **crest speed** that would leave the ball roughly **12 to 18 inches past** if the lower section were flat.

This is the part golfers miss.

You are not blasting it to the crest.
You are trying to let the ball **arrive at the break point with manners** so the lower tier can do the rest.

### 3. Putting across a ridge or double-break tier

If the putt changes direction after the ridge, I want the ball crossing into the second section with a pace that would finish about **18 to 24 inches past** if the second tier were the whole green.

That gives you enough motion to hold line without letting the second slope turn the putt into a runaway.

## The First-Landing-Zone Map

Use these defaults.

### Up a tier

Land the putt:

- **1 to 2 feet onto the upper tier** on moderate slopes
- **2 to 3 feet onto the upper tier** when the climb is more severe or the green is slower

Anything shorter usually leaves you on the wrong floor.

### Down a tier

Treat the crest like a launch point.

I want the ball:

- reaching the crest with soft pace
- crossing it without a jab
- and entering the lower tier with enough energy to keep tracking but not enough to race

Mentally, your first target is often **right on the ridge** or **6 to 12 inches beyond it**, depending on how sharply the surface falls away.

### Across a tiered double-break

Split the putt in half.

Pick:

- the first high point or entry line
- then the second-tier lane where the ball should start feeding

If you do not know where the ball needs to enter the second tier, you do not actually have a read yet.

You have a guess with posture.

## Three Tiered Putt Types and the Right Play

## 1. Uphill to a back shelf

This is the classic destination-golf humbler.

Plan:

- use the pace window of **18 to 30 inches past**
- read only enough first-section break to arrive on line at the crest
- picture the ball **finishing onto the upper shelf**, not just touching it

This is where [how to putt uphill without leaving it short](/blog/how-to-putt-uphill-without-leaving-it-short/) still matters. Tiered greens do not replace uphill putting rules. They just make lazy pace look worse.

## 2. Downhill off a shelf

This is the one that turns normal humans into steering disasters.

Plan:

- get the ball to the ridge with **soft, controlled speed**
- let gravity handle the second half
- accept that the safe leave is often **below the hole** and a little short, not pin-high chaos

If slick downhill pace is the part ruining you, stack this with [how to putt downhill without three-putting](/blog/how-to-putt-downhill-without-three-putting/). A tier just adds one more decision point. The penalty for hitting it like an idiot is still the same.

## 3. Sidehill into a second break

This is the fake double-break that gets over-read all the time.

Usually the right read is not "huge circus putt."
It is:

- modest first break
- a clean entry point into tier two
- and speed that lets the second section actually influence the roll

If you hammer the first section, the second section never gets a vote.
That is why so many tiered putts miss low on the last third. The golfer did not under-read it. They over-hit it.

That same miss pattern is why [how to putt sidehill putts without overplaying break](/blog/how-to-putt-sidehill-putts-without-overplaying-break/) and [how to read greens better without guessing](/blog/how-to-read-greens-better-without-guessing/) pair so well here.

## The 20-Second Tiered-Green Routine

This is the whole on-course routine.

1. Decide whether the putt goes **up a tier**, **down a tier**, or **across two slopes**.
2. Pick the **first landing zone**.
3. Pick the **crest speed window**.
4. Pick the **entry lane** onto the final tier if the break changes.
5. Roll it with one pace picture and stop adding drama.

If you are standing over the ball still debating all three sections at once, the read is already cooked.

## The Misses That Tell You What Went Wrong

### Miss 1: It dies on the ridge

That usually means:

- you played the crest like a stopping point
- or your pace window was too careful

This is the most common amateur miss on tiered greens.

### Miss 2: It gets over the ridge and keeps trucking

That usually means:

- the crest speed was too hot
- or you forgot the lower tier was going to add speed

You did not "get unlucky."
You treated a staircase like a flat hallway.

### Miss 3: It misses low on the second break

Usually:

- you hit the first section too firm
- which reduced the total break
- and the ball entered tier two like a bullet instead of a putt

Again, that is not mostly a read problem.
It is a pace problem pretending to be a line problem.

## Drill 1: The Crest Ladder

Find a practice putt that climbs onto a second tier from **20 to 30 feet**.

Set two tees:

- one **12 inches** onto the upper tier
- one **30 inches** onto the upper tier

Hit **10 balls**.

Rules:

- every ball must get onto the upper tier
- every miss must finish between those tees
- goal: **7 of 10**

This teaches you the pace window instead of the usual golfer nonsense of celebrating the one perfect ball and learning nothing from the other nine.

## Drill 2: The Drop-Over Gate

Find a putt that rolls off one shelf onto another.

Set:

- one tee right at the crest
- one tee **12 inches** past the crest on the lower tier

Hit **8 balls** trying to roll through that gate and finish inside **3 feet**.

Benchmarks:

- **6 of 8** through the gate is solid
- **5 of 8 or worse** means the ridge is still controlling you instead of the other way around

## Drill 3: The 9-Ball Tiered-Greens Circuit

Use three putts:

- one up a tier
- one down a tier
- one across a tiered double-break

Hit **3 balls** from each station.

Track:

- did it reach the correct first landing zone
- did it enter the next tier with the intended speed
- did it finish inside **3 feet**

Goals:

- **7 of 9** inside three feet is strong
- **6 of 9** is playable
- **5 of 9 or worse** means your first putt is still too random on multi-level greens

## What I Want You Tracking for the Next Five Rounds

Write down every tiered-green first putt over **20 feet**.

Track:

- putt type: up, down, or across
- whether you chose a first landing zone
- whether it got to the right tier correctly
- distance of the second putt

Goals:

- **zero** putts dying on the wrong tier
- average second putt of **3 feet or less**
- no more than **one three-putt** from tiered greens over five rounds

If the second putt keeps staying at **5 to 7 feet**, your pace window is still too wild.

## Bottom Line

Tiered greens get easier the second you stop trying to read them like one big normal putt.

Use the **first-landing-zone system**:

- pick the first zone
- pick the crest speed
- let the next tier do its share of the work

Do that, and tiered greens stop feeling like random cruelty and start feeling like a speed-control problem you can actually solve.
