I played a round last summer where I hit the ball terribly—thin irons, pushed drives, fat wedges, the whole disaster catalog—and shot 74.

The week before, I felt great on the range and shot 82 because I tried to turn every decent swing into permission to attack.

That is the point of course management: **your strategy has to survive the swing you brought to the course**, not the swing you hoped would show up.

You do not need 40 clever rules. You need a repeatable decision process that answers three questions before every full shot:

1. **What score am I protecting on this hole?**
2. **Where is the penalty or double-bogey miss?**
3. **What target gives my normal shot pattern room to exist?**

This guide turns those questions into an actual system.

## The Course-Management Decision Tree

Run this before a tee shot or approach:

### Step 1: Is there a penalty inside your normal miss pattern?

- **No:** play your stock club and stock shape.
- **Yes:** continue to Step 2.

### Step 2: Can a shorter club remove the penalty without making the next shot unreasonable?

- **Yes:** use the shorter club.
- **No:** continue to Step 3.

### Step 3: Can you aim far enough away that your typical miss stays playable?

- **Yes:** move the target, not your swing.
- **No:** choose the recovery route and accept the extra shot.

That final answer is where most doubles disappear. Taking one extra shot on purpose is often cheaper than bringing water, out-of-bounds, trees, or a short-sided bunker into play.

## Match the Strategy to Your Scoring Goal

“Play smart” means different things depending on the score you are trying to shoot.

| Scoring goal | Primary objective | What to avoid | Practical target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Break 100 | Keep the ball advancing | penalties, two failed shots in a row | get near the green in regulation plus one |
| Break 90 | Eliminate doubles | hero recoveries and short-sided misses | bogey is acceptable; pars are gains |
| Break 80 | Protect pars | penalties, three-putts, missed greens in terrible places | center-green patterns and planned aggression |
| Shoot near par | Create selective birdie chances | emotional aggression after mistakes | attack only when lie, number, and miss all agree |

If you are trying to break 90, follow the full [break-90 scorecard plan](/blog/how-to-break-90-for-real/). If the target is 79, use the stricter benchmarks in [how to break 80](/blog/how-to-break-80/).

## Tee-Shot Strategy: Choose a Landing Zone, Not a Club

Most golfers decide “driver” before they look at the hole. Reverse that order.

Start with the landing zone:

- Where does the fairway become narrow?
- At what distance does a bunker, water hazard, or dogleg enter play?
- Which side gives the cleanest angle to the green?
- Does your normal miss finish in grass or in a penalty?

Then choose the club that reaches the widest useful part of the hole.

### Example: 380-yard par 4 with water at 260

If driver brings the water into your normal pattern and 3-wood leaves 140 yards from the fairway, the question is not “Can I hit driver past it?” The question is whether the extra 20–30 yards materially improve the approach enough to justify the penalty risk.

Usually they do not.

Our [fairway-finder tee-shot plan](/blog/fairway-finder-tee-shot-plan/) gives you a simple version for days when the driver pattern is wider than normal.

## Approach Strategy: Stop Treating Every Flag the Same

Before choosing the target, give the pin a color:

| Pin type | Conditions | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Green | Centered pin, no severe nearby trouble, good lie and number | Flag or a small safe bias |
| Yellow | Pin near one edge with room on the opposite side | Middle-to-safe-side |
| Red | Tucked over trouble, short-sided miss, poor lie, or awkward number | Center of green |

The useful test is the **15-foot miss check**:

> If the shot finishes 15 feet left, right, short, or long of the target, how many of those misses remain playable?

If one normal miss produces a bunker shot with no green, a penalty, or a recovery over a ridge, move the target. The complete version is in [stop short-siding yourself](/blog/stop-short-siding-yourself-approach-strategy/).

## Know Your Real Shot Pattern

Do not plan around your best strike or your worst disaster. Plan around the middle of your pattern.

Hit 20 shots with a representative iron:

1. Ignore the three best and three worst.
2. Mark where the remaining 14 finished.
3. Find the center and width of that group.
4. Use that width when choosing on-course targets.

If those 14 shots cover 20 yards left-to-right, a flag five yards from a bunker is not an aggressive target. It is a target that pretends your dispersion does not exist.

Your pattern can also change during the round. If the first six holes show a consistent push, acknowledge it. Do not spend the back nine aiming as if a range correction has already worked.

## Par-5 Strategy: Use Three Go/No-Go Checks

Reaching a par 5 in two is not automatically the correct play. Go only when all three checks pass:

1. **The lie is clean enough** to produce predictable contact.
2. **The carry is inside a reliable number**, not a personal record.
3. **The worst normal miss remains playable.**

If one fails, lay up to a distance you can actually calibrate.

### Example

You have 235 yards to a green with water short and a bunker long. Your best 3-wood can reach, but the normal pattern includes a 210-yard carry and a low left miss.

That is a layup. Choose the widest landing area that leaves a stock wedge—not the nearest fairway yardage that looks ambitious. See [how to lay up on par 5s](/blog/how-to-lay-up-on-par-5s/) for the full distance-first system.

## Recovery Shots: End the Disaster With One Swing

A bad drive does not have to cause a bad hole. The expensive mistake is trying to erase it immediately.

Use this recovery hierarchy:

1. Can I advance the ball to a full-shot position with a low-risk swing?
2. If not, can I return it to the fairway?
3. What play guarantees that the next swing is normal?

Punching sideways can feel like surrender. It is often the move that keeps bogey available. Our [recovery-shot strategy](/blog/recovery-shot-strategy-stop-doubles/) is built specifically around stopping one mistake from becoming three.

## Trouble Holes: Set a Score Before You Hit

Every course has holes where the architecture, wind, or your shot shape creates a bad matchup.

Set the goal before the tee shot:

- On a scoreable hole, create a birdie look without risking double.
- On a neutral hole, make a routine par decision.
- On a survival hole, keep bogey as the worst likely result.

This prevents the common mistake of playing every hole at the same aggression level.

If a 440-yard par 4 into the wind is a survival hole for you, a tee shot in play, an approach short of trouble, and one chip plus two putts is not failure. It is a planned five that protects the round.

## Wind: Change Club and Target Before Changing Swing

Wind magnifies spin and curve. The first adjustment should be more club and a controlled swing, not an attempt to manufacture a new shot shape under pressure.

- **Headwind:** take more club, swing within yourself, and allow extra room for curve.
- **Tailwind:** expect a flatter landing and more release.
- **Crosswind:** start with the normal shape and widen the safe target on the downwind side.

The full matrices belong in [how to play golf in the wind](/blog/how-to-play-golf-in-the-wind/); this page's rule is simpler: do not let wind talk you into a swing you did not bring.

## A Five-Minute Pre-Round Plan

You do not need a yardage book covered in equations.

Before the round:

1. Mark the holes where driver brings a penalty into play.
2. Mark the par 5s with a forced carry near the green.
3. Identify the three hardest holes and accept bogey in advance.
4. Pick one side of the green you refuse to miss on each par 3.
5. Write down the score goal and the maximum number of penalties you will tolerate.

For a break-90 round, that might mean:

- zero penalty strokes
- zero hero recoveries
- no three-putts from inside 30 feet
- bogey or better on 15 holes

That plan is specific enough to guide decisions and simple enough to remember.

## Track the Mistakes Strategy Can Fix

Fairways and putts are useful, but a course-management review needs different categories:

- penalty strokes
- shots blocked by trees or hazards
- short-sided misses
- failed recovery attempts
- three-putts caused by attacking the wrong section
- par-5 go-for-it attempts and outcomes

After five rounds, look for the repeat offender. If penalties lead the list, fix tee targets. If short-sided misses lead, fix approach targets. If failed recoveries lead, strengthen the chip-out rule.

## The Bottom Line

Good course management is not playing scared. It is spending aggression where the expected reward is worth the miss.

Before every meaningful shot, decide:

1. the score you are protecting,
2. the trouble you are removing,
3. and the target that fits your actual pattern.

The swing will still misbehave. The scorecard does not have to follow it.
