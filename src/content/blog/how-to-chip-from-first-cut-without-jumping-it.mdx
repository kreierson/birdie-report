The first cut is where golfers talk themselves into stupid little half-lies.

It is not thick enough to scream **rough**.
It is not clean enough to behave like fairway.

So players do the most predictable thing possible:

They use the same stock chip they use from tight grass, then act offended when the ball comes out hotter, lower, and ten feet past the hole.

That is the whole trap.

**First-cut chips are not hard because they are dramatic. They are hard because they are sneaky.**

If the ball is sitting down badly, you need [how to chip from greenside rough](/blog/how-to-chip-from-greenside-rough/). If it is sitting clean on bare or clipped turf, use [how to chip tight lies without blading it](/blog/how-to-chip-tight-lies-without-blading-it/). This piece is for the in-between lie where there is just enough grass to change the strike, but not enough to make you respect it automatically.

## The First Rule: Expect More Jump and More Roll

From the first cut, the ball usually comes out:

- a little faster
- a little lower
- and with less predictable friction than a tight-lie chip

That means your default expectation should be:

- **1 to 2 yards less check**
- **3 to 6 feet more rollout**

That is the number range I want in your head before the club even goes back.

If you keep planning the shot like a fairway chip, you will keep landing it too deep and watching it scamper like it owes somebody money.

## Use the Ball-Back, Softer-Handle Setup

Here is the setup I trust most from the first cut:

- ball **one ball back of center**
- pressure **60 to 65 percent** on the lead foot
- handle just **barely** ahead, not shoved
- stance slightly open
- grip pressure around **4 out of 10**

That softer-handle part matters.

A lot of golfers respond to first-cut grass by leaning the shaft way forward and trying to "make sure" they catch it clean.

That usually does one of two things:

- turns the strike too steep and grabby
- or exposes too much leading edge and makes contact weirdly hot

You want enough forward structure to stay organized.
You do **not** want the club arriving like it is trying to punish the turf.

## Pick Less Loft Than Your Fear Wants

This is where most chips go wrong.

The lie looks uncertain, so golfers add loft to feel safer.
That usually makes the strike less predictable.

My default first-cut menu:

| Situation | Club | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plenty of green, simple chip | **8-iron or 9-iron** | easiest rollout and best contact margin |
| Standard stock chip | **PW or gap wedge** | enough loft without getting too cute |
| Need a little carry but still some release | **54 or 56 degree** | useful when rough sits between you and the front edge |
| Tight pin, little green, ball sitting clean | **58 degree** only if necessary | higher risk, smaller margin |

The key is simple:

**Use the lowest loft that clears the grass in front and lands on the green early.**

That is the same adult logic behind [chip-shot technique around the green](/blog/chip-shot-technique-around-the-green/) and [save more pars after missed greens](/blog/save-more-pars-after-missed-greens/). The easy shot should stay easy.

## The Landing Spot Needs to Move Closer to You

This is the adjustment that saves the most shots.

Because the first cut tends to produce a hotter, lower release, I want the landing spot coming back closer to the front edge of the green.

Start here:

- if you would normally land it **6 paces on**, try **4 to 5 paces on**
- if you would normally land it **4 paces on**, try **3 paces on**
- if the chip is mostly rollout, move the landing spot back about **15 to 20 percent**

Example:

If your stock fairway chip lands **9 feet** onto the green, start the first-cut version around **7 to 7.5 feet** on instead.

Not because it is fancy.
Because the lie is already telling you the ball will release more.

## What the Motion Should Feel Like

From the first cut, I want:

- a shorter backswing
- a chest-led motion
- and a shallow brush through the grass

My favorite feel is:

**clip it and keep walking.**

That means:

- no stabbing down
- no trying to help it up
- no late wrist flip to rescue contact

The club should move through the grass like it is skimming the top of it, not excavating a trench.

If you get too steep, the grass grabs the club and the strike gets dead or grabby.
If you try to lift it, the ball jumps.

Neither one helps.

## The Three First-Cut Lies You Need to Recognize

### 1. Ball sitting up on top

This is the lie that launches hotter than you expect.

Plan:

- less loft
- earlier landing spot
- smaller motion

This is usually more flyer than rough.

### 2. Ball sitting half-down

This is the lie that confuses people most.

Plan:

- keep pressure forward
- use enough loft to clear the grass
- accept that it may come out with less spin and a little more rollout

This is the stock "do not get cute" lie.

### 3. Ball sitting clean but grain leaning into you

This can make the grass feel grabby even though the lie looks decent.

Plan:

- slightly firmer motion
- earlier landing spot
- less face manipulation

If grain is the bigger problem, also read [how to chip into grain without leaving it short](/blog/how-to-chip-into-grain-without-leaving-it-short/). A lot of first-cut lies become grain problems wearing a fake mustache.

## The Mistakes That Keep Showing Up

### 1. Landing it too deep

This is the classic one.

Golfers use their fairway landing spot from a lie that clearly will not check the same way.
Then they blame the green.

No.
It was the plan.

### 2. Adding loft because the lie feels uncertain

More loft is not automatically safer.

Often it just shrinks your contact window and adds speed if the ball comes out clean.

### 3. Trying to chop down to "beat the grass"

That impulse makes sense until you actually do it.

Then the club grabs, the spin disappears, and the ball either dies or rockets.

### 4. Decelerating because you are afraid of the jumper

First-cut chips need structure, not fear.

If you slow down late, contact gets even less predictable.

## My Rollout Baseline

Until you build your own personal numbers, use this quick starting map:

| Club | Typical first-cut rollout expectation |
| --- | --- |
| **8-iron** | roughly **3x to 4x** carry |
| **PW** | roughly **2x to 3x** carry |
| **54/56** | roughly **1x to 2x** carry |

Those are not perfect laws.
They are starting lanes.

What matters is that the first cut usually nudges the shot toward **more rollout than your fairway version**, not less.

## Drill 1: The Two-Towel Landing Test

Put one towel at your normal fairway landing spot.
Put a second towel **15 to 20 percent shorter**.

Hit:

- **5 balls** from tight grass
- **5 balls** from the first cut

Use the same club.

Track which towel produces the better finish from each lie.

You are teaching yourself the difference instead of pretending both lies deserve the same picture.

## Drill 2: The 9-Ball First-Cut Ladder

Drop **9 balls** in the first cut around one green.

Hit:

- **3 with an 8- or 9-iron**
- **3 with a pitching wedge**
- **3 with a 54 or 56**

Scoring:

- **2 points** inside **6 feet**
- **1 point** inside **12 feet**
- **0 points** outside **12 feet**

Maximum score: **18**

Benchmarks:

- **12+** means you have a usable stock system
- **9-11** means contact is fine but landing spots need work
- **8 or worse** means you are still guessing

## Drill 3: The One-Lie, Three-Club Test

This is the fastest on-course prep drill I know.

Take one first-cut lie and hit:

1. low-release chip with **8-iron**
2. stock chip with **PW**
3. softer carry version with **54 or 56**

Then ask:

- which one asked the least of you
- which one produced the most predictable rollout
- which one you would actually trust on the course

That last answer matters most.

The "best" club in theory does not matter if you only trust it on the range.

## What I Would Do on the Course

### Front pin with room

Use the safer club and land it early.

I would much rather leave a putt from **8 feet below the hole** than fly one pin-high and watch it jog away.

### Middle pin

This is the stock-pitching-wedge spot for most golfers.

Land it just short of your normal fairway landing spot and let the ball do the boring thing.

### Short-sided pin

Now the first cut becomes expensive.

If the lie is even slightly uncertain, I want the safer side of the green unless the carry window is obvious.
This is where [short-sided strategy](/blog/how-to-play-front-pins-without-making-bogey/) still applies. The lie does not owe you hero-spin just because the hole location is rude.

## Bottom Line

The first cut is not a normal chip and not a full rough chop.
It is the awkward middle child that punishes lazy assumptions.

Use the simple system:

- ball **one ball back**
- pressure **60 to 65 percent** forward
- softer handle
- lower loft when possible
- landing spot moved back **15 to 20 percent**

Then practice it with real landing spots instead of vibes.

Do that, and the first cut stops being that annoying little lie that turns routine pars into "how the hell did that go twelve feet by?"
