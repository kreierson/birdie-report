The basic chip is not a miniature full swing. It is a low-speed shot built to produce clean contact, land on a chosen patch of green, and roll a predictable distance.

Most golfers make it harder by choosing the club first—usually a lob wedge—then trying to invent a motion that fits the shot.

Use the opposite order:

1. Read the lie.
2. Choose the landing spot.
3. Estimate the rollout.
4. Select the least-lofted club that can clear the obstacle and finish near the hole.
5. Make one committed motion.

That sequence is the foundation of reliable chipping.

## The Stock Chipping Setup

Use this as the baseline for a normal lie with no unusual obstacle:

1. **Narrow stance:** heels roughly a clubhead apart.
2. **Ball near center:** move it only slightly back for a lower runner.
3. **Pressure favoring the lead foot:** enough to keep the low point forward without leaning dramatically.
4. **Handle slightly ahead:** modest shaft lean, not an extreme press.
5. **Grip down:** shorten the club for control.
6. **Chest and arms move together:** let the body rotation carry the handle through.

The goal is to brush the turf at or just after the ball. You do not need to trap it with a violent downward hit, and you do not need to scoop it into the air.

## The Motion: A Putting Stroke With Some Body Turn

For the stock chip:

- keep wrist action quiet,
- move the chest with the arms,
- let the clubhead stay low through the strike,
- and finish with the chest slightly open to the target.

The backswing length controls distance. The through-swing should be at least as committed as the backswing.

This motion is intentionally boring. Once contact and rollout are predictable, you can add hinge, speed, or loft for specialized shots.

## Read the Lie Before Choosing the Club

The lie decides how much certainty you have.

| Lie | What it changes | Safer starting choice |
|---|---|---|
| Tight fairway or collar | little grass under the ball; thin contact is exposed | putter, hybrid, 8-iron, or PW with minimal shaft lean |
| First cut | ball usually sits cleanly but may jump slightly | PW through sand wedge depending on carry |
| Sitting up in rough | club can slide under the ball and add loft | choke down, keep the chest moving, expect less reliable contact |
| Buried in rough | grass grabs the hosel and reduces speed | more loft, firmer grip, steeper strike, expect rollout |
| Into the grain | turf resists the leading edge | more bounce, less forward press, committed motion |
| Bare or muddy lie | very small contact margin | putter or low-loft option when the route is available |

## Choose the Landing Spot Before the Club

Pick a flat patch on the green whenever possible. Landing on fringe, a downslope, or the crest of a ridge makes the first bounce harder to predict.

Walk through three questions:

1. What must the ball carry?
2. Where is the first flat landing area?
3. How much green remains after that spot?

Then choose the club that creates the required carry-to-roll pattern.

### Example 1: Five feet of fringe, 35 feet of green

There is no reason to fly the ball halfway to the hole. Land an 8-iron or 9-iron a few feet onto the green and let it roll.

### Example 2: Twelve feet to the green, 20 feet to the pin

A pitching wedge or gap wedge can clear the fringe and still release. The exact club depends on slope and green speed.

### Example 3: Eight feet to the green, pin six feet on

The landing area is small. Use more loft only if the lie supports it. If the ball is buried or the green runs away, the honest goal may be 10 feet rather than a tap-in.

### Example 4: Bunker between the ball and the hole

This is no longer a stock chip. Use a higher-lofted pitch if the lie allows, or accept a safer section of the green. The [flop-shot guide](/blog/how-to-hit-a-flop-shot/) explains when the high option is actually available.

## A Practical Club-Selection Ladder

Start at the lowest-risk option and move up only when the shot requires it:

1. **Putter** if the surface between ball and green is predictable.
2. **Hybrid or fairway wood** when the collar is sticky but the shot can stay low.
3. **7- through 9-iron** for maximum rollout.
4. **Pitching or gap wedge** for a balanced carry-and-roll shot.
5. **Sand or lob wedge** when an obstacle or short landing area requires height.

This is not a rule that lower loft is always correct. It is a rule that extra loft must solve a real problem.

## The Landing-to-Roll Calibration Drill

Generic ratios such as “one-third carry, two-thirds roll” are only starting points. Loft, ball, green speed, slope, and contact change the result.

Build your own chart:

1. Place a towel or three-foot circle five paces onto a practice green.
2. Hit five balls each with a 9-iron, pitching wedge, gap wedge, and sand wedge.
3. Land every ball in the same zone.
4. Record the average rollout in paces.
5. Repeat uphill and downhill.

You now have real rollout references for your equipment instead of a ratio borrowed from somebody else's green.

## Distance Control: Change One Variable

Once you have selected the club, keep the setup and tempo stable. Change the length of the motion.

- Short chip: clubhead just outside the trail foot.
- Medium chip: hands reach roughly mid-thigh.
- Long chip: slightly longer motion with more body rotation.

Do not add a sudden hit at the bottom. If the required carry makes the motion feel too long for a chip, switch to the [8–15 yard pitch-shot system](/blog/how-to-control-8-to-15-yard-pitch-shots/).

## Troubleshooting Common Chipping Misses

| Miss | Likely cause | First correction |
|---|---|---|
| Chunked behind the ball | pressure drifting back or motion stopping | keep pressure forward and turn chest through |
| Bladed across the green | rising through impact or excessive forward press on a tight lie | soften shaft lean and keep chest level |
| Ball comes out dead | buried lie, grass between face and ball, or deceleration | add loft and speed; plan for more rollout |
| Ball jumps too far | flyer lie or too little loft for the landing area | expect less spin and land it shorter |
| Starts right | handle stalls while face stays open | rotate chest and handle through together |
| Pulls left | hands flip or shoulders aim left | quiet wrists and check alignment |
| Good contact, wrong distance | club/landing combination was wrong | recalibrate rollout rather than changing mechanics |

If chunks are the repeating problem, use the more focused [stop chunking chip shots](/blog/how-to-stop-chunking-chip-shots/) diagnostic.

## Two Practice Games That Transfer

### Landing-Spot Ladder

Choose three landing zones at three, six, and nine paces.

- Hit three balls to each zone with one club.
- Score one point for landing inside a three-foot circle.
- Repeat with a second club.
- Try to beat the score without changing the target size.

This separates carry control from whatever happens after the first bounce.

### Nine-Ball Up-and-Down Test

Drop one ball in nine different lies:

- three fairway/tight lies,
- three first-cut lies,
- three rough or awkward lies.

Play every ball into the hole and record the total strokes. One ball per lie forces you to read and commit like you would on the course.

## The Pre-Shot Routine

Use the same sequence every time:

1. Inspect how the ball sits.
2. Read slope from landing spot to hole.
3. Pick the landing spot.
4. Choose the club for the rollout.
5. Rehearse the motion while looking at the landing spot.
6. Step in and go.

Indecision causes more bad chips than a lack of shot options. You need one committed plan, not four possibilities standing over the ball.

## The Bottom Line

Reliable chipping comes from matching the shot to the lie:

- use a narrow, stable setup,
- land the ball on a predictable patch,
- choose loft based on the required rollout,
- and make a committed motion that keeps the chest moving.
