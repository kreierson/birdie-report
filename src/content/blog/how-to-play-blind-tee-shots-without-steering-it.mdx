Blind tee shots do a weird thing to otherwise functional golfers.

The fairway disappears.
The landing area turns theoretical.
And suddenly the swing gets infected by all the worst instincts at once:

- aim vaguely
- swing harder
- try to help it
- then act surprised when the ball starts on the wrong line and finishes in whatever ugly thing the hole was hiding from you

That is the blind-tee-shot spiral.

Most golfers do **not** butcher blind tee shots because the hole is impossible.
They butcher them because they treat "I cannot see the landing area" like a reason to abandon process.

It is not.

If anything, blind tee shots demand **more** discipline, not less.

## The Rule First: Blind Tee Shots Are Start-Line Shots, Not Hero-Line Shots

Here is the whole article in one sentence:

**On a blind tee shot, your job is to start the ball in the correct window with a stock swing.**

Not carve it around imagined trouble.
Not hit the hardest driver of the day because the hole feels mysterious.
Not manufacture some brave little shape because the caddie said "anything up that chimney is fine."

I want you thinking:

- what is the true start line?
- how wide is the safe window?
- what club lets me start it there most often?

If you need the bigger tee-shot framework first, go read [the fairway-finder tee-shot plan](/blog/fairway-finder-tee-shot-plan/), [how to play doglegs without getting greedy](/blog/how-to-play-doglegs-without-getting-greedy/), and [how to hit 3-wood off the tee on tight holes](/blog/how-to-hit-3-wood-off-the-tee-on-tight-holes/). This piece is for the specific problem where the hole hides the landing area and your brain starts freelancing.

## Use the Landmark-and-12-Yard Window

This is the system:

1. Pick a **landmark** above or beyond the hidden landing area.
2. Pick the club that can start inside a **12-yard safe window** most often.
3. Commit to a stock shot shape.
4. Swing at the landmark, not at your fear.

That **12-yard window** is the number that matters.

Why 12 yards?

Because blind tee shots get dumb when the target becomes too precise for the swing you actually own.

If you cannot picture a safe start window at least about **12 yards wide**, one of two things is usually true:

- you picked the wrong club
- or you picked the wrong line

For a lot of golfers, that means clubbing down before ego writes another check.

## The Landmark Has to Be Real, Not Vague

Do not aim at "the right side."
Do not aim at "that general tree area."

Pick something you can actually commit to:

- the left edge of a distant tree
- the top of a bunker lip
- a chimney, tower, or hillside break
- or a dark patch in the background that sits on the correct launch line

The landmark does not have to be in the fairway.
It has to sit on the **same line** as the fairway window you want.

That is the whole trick.

You are replacing missing fairway visibility with a visible external line.

## Pick the Club With the Best Start-Line Percentage, Not the Coolest Distance

This is where blind holes expose dumb bag choices fast.

Ask these three questions:

1. **Which club can I start on the intended line most often?**
2. **Does driver actually improve the next shot enough to justify the miss pattern?**
3. **If I miss slightly, is the result still in play or immediately stupid?**

That is the same adult filtering from [how to save a round when driver is gone](/blog/how-to-save-a-round-when-driver-is-gone/) and [how to play long par 4s without making doubles](/blog/how-to-play-long-par-4s-without-making-doubles/).

My default blind-hole rule:

- if the hidden landing area is effectively **30 yards or wider** and the miss is mostly light rough, driver can stay in play
- if the real window is more like **20 to 30 yards** or the miss brings trees, cross bunkers, or punch-out golf, use the fairway finder
- if the miss is penalty trouble and the line still feels uncertain, club down again

Blind does not mean bomb away.
Blind usually means **fewer reasons to be reckless**.

## The 15-Second Blind-Tee-Shot Routine

This is the on-course script:

1. Stand behind the ball and identify the landmark.
2. Name the stock shot: straight, little fade, or little draw.
3. Confirm the start window is at least **12 yards wide**.
4. Pick the club that fits that window.
5. Step in and make a normal swing.

That is it.

If you are still standing over the ball imagining six disasters, you are not gathering more information.
You are just marinating in doubt.

## Three Blind Tee Shot Types and the Right Play

### 1. Blind over a rise, fairway mostly straight

This is the common "I cannot see it but the hole is not actually doing anything weird" version.

Plan:

- pick one background landmark
- use your stock shape
- and favor the club you start straightest

This is the easiest version.
Do not make it dramatic.

### 2. Blind tee shot on a dogleg

This is where golfers start inventing miracle lines.

Plan:

- aim at the **entry line** to the dogleg, not the prettiest fantasy corner
- give yourself at least **8 to 12 yards** of shape margin away from the dead side
- and use the club that keeps the ball below your maximum-curvature nonsense

If this is the exact shot type that keeps baiting you, pair this with [how to play doglegs without getting greedy](/blog/how-to-play-doglegs-without-getting-greedy/).

### 3. Blind tee shot with wind

This is the one that really punishes indecision.

Plan:

- shift the start line for the wind before you step in
- keep the swing length normal
- and do **not** swing harder just because the landing area is hidden

That is how wipey blocks and overcooked doubles start.

If the wind is the part turning you stupid, go straight to [how to play golf in the wind](/blog/how-to-play-golf-in-the-wind/) and [how to play crosswinds without overcorrecting](/blog/how-to-play-crosswinds-without-overcorrecting/).

## The Misses That Tell You What Went Wrong

### Miss 1: Pulled into the hidden trouble

Usually means:

- you got quick because the hole felt uncertain
- or you aimed too far away from the trouble and then overcorrected the swing

### Miss 2: Push or block into the safe-side rough

This is not always a disaster.
Sometimes it is a fine miss.

But if it keeps happening, you probably:

- picked too much club
- or chose a landmark that made you steer instead of swing

### Miss 3: Ball finishes in trouble on the "good" line anyway

Usually:

- the window was narrower than you admitted
- or driver was always the wrong club and you just wanted to feel aggressive

That is not bad luck.
That is bad pre-shot math.

## Drill 1: The Landmark Gate Drill

This is the fastest way to make blind-hole alignment less fictional.

### Setup

- Pick a range target or distant object
- Create a gate with two alignment sticks or range targets about **12 yards apart** at your landing distance
- Pick a background landmark directly behind the center of that gate

### Rules

- Hit **10 tee shots**
- Every ball has to start inside the gate
- Goal: **7 of 10**

You are training the exact skill blind tee shots demand: start-line ownership.

## Drill 2: The Club-Down Comparison Test

This is for golfers who always assume driver is still the answer.

### Setup

- Pick one target line
- Hit **5 drivers**
- Hit **5 fairway-finder clubs**

### Rules

- Count how many balls start inside the same **12-yard** window
- Count how many would still be clearly in play

If the fairway finder wins by **2 balls or more**, that is the club you should be using on the next real blind hole until driver proves it deserves the job again.

## Drill 3: The One-Ball Course-Management Rehearsal

This is the adult version.

On the range:

1. Pick a blind-hole scenario in your head.
2. Name the club.
3. Name the landmark.
4. Name the shot shape.
5. Hit **one ball**.

Do that **9 times** with different clubs and different fake hole shapes.

Goal:

- at least **6 of 9** start on the intended line

That drill pairs well with [how to practice golf with one ball](/blog/how-to-practice-golf-with-one-ball/) because blind-tee-shot golf is decision golf, not volume golf.

## What Good Blind-Tee-Shot Golf Actually Looks Like

It looks a lot less heroic than golfers want.

It looks like:

- picking a visible landmark fast
- taking the club that fits the width of the hole
- accepting the smart miss
- and making one normal swing

That is it.

The course hiding the fairway does not change your job.
It just punishes sloppy commitment faster.

## Bottom Line

If blind tee shots keep wrecking you, stop treating them like mystery missions.

Use:

- one real **landmark**
- one honest **12-yard start window**
- one club that fits that window
- and one stock swing

That is how blind tee shots turn back into golf instead of becoming a little panic ritual with spikes on.
